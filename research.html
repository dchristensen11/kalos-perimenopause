The research behind it

Every claim on the main page, with the study it came from. Including the places the specialists genuinely disagree, and the places this review is thin. You should be able to check our work.

The anchor study

SWAN: 1,246 women, annual DEXA scans

The Study of Women's Health Across the Nation followed women with annual body composition scans across the menopause transition, anchored to each woman's final period. Mean age at baseline 47.1, mean age at final period 52.2. It is the single best longitudinal dataset that exists on this question.

Before (8 to 2 yrs prior)TransitionAfter (1.5 to 10.5 yrs post)
Fat mass+1.0% / yr+1.7% / yrFlat. Zero slope
Lean mass+0.2% / yr−0.2% / yrStabilises
Total attributable to the transition itself: roughly 3.5 lb of fat and 0.4 lb of lean mass. The authors also note that total bodyweight gain does not accelerate during the transition. Corroborated by the SWAN six year analysis (PMC2714766): +7.5 lb fat, −0.5 lb skeletal muscle over six years.

On the claim that women lose 10 percent of their muscle through menopause

Not supported

This is the figure circulating hardest on social media and the longitudinal data does not support it. Actual loss runs under 1 percent per year, it is similar in men and women, and it happens overwhelmingly in people who do not train.

Most of the literature cited for the frightening numbers is cross sectional, meaning it compares 45 year olds to 65 year olds, rather than longitudinal, meaning it follows the same women through the transition. Cross sectional designs confuse ageing, generational differences, and the collapse in physical activity that happens across those decades.

Where they disagree

Six genuine fault lines

These are the arguments you have probably seen online. On each one, here is what both sides actually claim and which position the evidence supports. Several of them turn out to be much narrower than the noise around them suggests.

1. Heavy and low rep, or just hard?

Narrower than it looks

The slogan: "lift heavy", framed as a female specific requirement, with the implication that moderate rep work stops working after menopause.

What Sims actually prescribes: a minimum of three resistance sessions a week with compound movements, at RPE 8 or 2 to 3 reps in reserve, and explicitly not to failure. The Diary of a CEO roundtable with Wright lands on 2 to 4 days a week at 70 to 80 percent of a one rep max, stopping short of failure.

What the evidence camp says: as long as it is genuinely challenging, it works. Strength and muscle respond across a wide rep range provided you get close enough to failure and keep progressing.

Verdict: there is effectively no actionable disagreement here. 70 to 80 percent at 2 to 3 reps in reserve is roughly 8 to 12 reps, which is ordinary hypertrophy programming, not the heavy triples the slogan implies. Both roads lead to the same session. The argument is about branding and about why it works, not about what goes on your program.

2. Cycle syncing

Debunk wins

The claim: program your training and nutrition around the follicular and luteal phases of your cycle.

The counter: Colenso-Semple's own work found no difference in muscle protein synthesis between the late follicular and mid luteal phases.

Verdict: unsupported, and for perimenopausal women it is moot anyway, because cycles have become irregular by definition. It becomes actively harmful when it hands you a reason to skip a session.

3. Protein: how much, and measured against what?

Genuine spread

This is a real three way disagreement, and the primary sources are further apart than the "eat more protein" consensus suggests. Below, every prescription converted for a 170 lb woman at 32 percent body fat, which is 115 lb of lean mass.

SourcePrescriptionWorks out to
Haver80 to 120 g/day, citing data at 1.5 to 1.7 g per kg of lean mass~78 to 89 g/day
Wright1.6 g per kg bodyweight, about 0.73 g per lb~123 g/day
KalosMidpoint of lean mass and total weight, in grams. Shortcut, 0.9 g per lb~143 g/day
Sims2.0 to 2.3 g per kg bodyweight, plus 40 to 60 g within 45 min of training~155 to 177 g/day
LyonAbout 1 g per lb bodyweight~170 g/day

What the trials show. A trial in overweight postmenopausal women in a calorie deficit compared 15 g, 35 g and 60 g of whey. 35 g was the effective dose. Not 15, and there was no additional benefit at 60. That is almost exactly the population this page is written for.

What they do not show. Timing. When total daily protein is matched, how you distribute it within the day does not change the outcome. The per meal threshold of roughly 30 g is real but modest.

The most useful point in this whole review: the stronger criticism of the highest prescriptions is the denominator, not the dose. Prescribing per kg of total bodyweight scales your target with your fat mass, so the higher your body fat, the more it overshoots. At 30 to 35 percent body fat that is a systematic error, and taking the midpoint of lean mass and total weight is exactly the correction for it.

Verdict: use the midpoint of lean mass and total weight off your scan, or 0.9 g per lb where there is no scan. Aim for 30 to 40 g at each of 3 or 4 meals, because that is the easiest route to the daily total, not because the clock matters. Honest framing: the highest published figures are a little high and calculated from the wrong starting point. The gap is one chicken breast, not a category error.

4. Creatine: muscle, or water?

Both are right

The enthusiastic camp: close to essential for women in midlife, sometimes at 5 to 10 g a day, sometimes framed as useful even without training.

The skeptical camp: the immediate jump in lean mass is intracellular water, not contractile tissue, and creatine is no substitute for lifting.

Verdict: both are right about different things. The meta-analysis in postmenopausal women found creatine improved leg press by about 16 lb, at 5 g a day or more, in combination with resistance training. Real, consistent, modest and conditional. 3 to 5 g daily, no loading phase. Expect 2 to 4 lb on the scale in week one and know that it is water inside the muscle.

5. Weighted vests, jumping and bone

Split

The claim: weighted vests worn through daily activity, plus jump training, for bone density.

The counter: oversold. The bone evidence for progressive resistance training is far stronger than for a vest worn while doing housework.

What the trial actually tested. LIFTMOR (Watson et al, 2018) ran brief, supervised, twice weekly high intensity resistance and impact training in postmenopausal women with osteopenia and osteoporosis. It beat a low intensity home program on bone mass at the hip and spine, plus hip geometry, height and physical function. Against the standing clinical caution, it produced no adverse events and no fractures, and it improved posture.

Verdict: impact loading for bone is legitimate and evidenced, but LIFTMOR was supervised heavy compound lifting plus deliberate impact. It is not evidence for a vest worn while doing chores, which is the specific prescription drawing the criticism. Do it as progressive loaded compound work plus a jumping cue. Vests worn through daily activity remain unevidenced and carry real injury risk in a deconditioned body.

6. Training fasted

Right instinct, wrong reason

The claim: perimenopausal women should never train fasted, because morning fasted training raises cortisol and sympathetic drive on top of an already elevated baseline.

The counter: the acute hormonal response is not the outcome. Cortisol does rise acutely, but a 2025 systematic review of fasted versus fed resistance training found that in women, the effects on body fat percentage were small, the effects on lean mass were trivial, and the differences between groups were trivial throughout.

Our position, split by session type. Trivial differences mean the trials could not detect a difference, not that equivalence is proven, and the confidence intervals leave room for a small real cost to muscle. When muscle is the primary target, the asymmetry favours eating: fuel your lifting sessions with 15 to 20 g of protein. The upside is better load and rep quality and the cost is negligible. Fasted is fine for walking and easy cardio, where fat loss is the only job of the session. And if you have trained fasted at 6am for fifteen years and you are progressing, this is not worth changing.

Bonus: hormone therapy as a body composition tool

Outside our scope

Marketed as: the fix for midlife weight gain.

The evidence: modest and mixed. A meta-analysis found a small and inconsistent effect on muscle mass. The OsteoLaus study found hormone therapy users had lower visceral fat and lower BMI, around 0.9 kg per square metre. Those are genuine effects, particularly on where fat sits.

Verdict: hormone therapy is a legitimate treatment for menopausal symptoms with a modest favourable effect on fat distribution. It is not a body composition intervention and it will not take you from 32 percent to 25 percent. That decision belongs to you and your doctor, and it is outside our scope to recommend either way.

How much we actually eat

The uncomfortable one
PMID 16321603Under reporting got worse after women joined a weight loss program, from 39.7 percent to 60.3 percent of women under reporting.
PMID 8107549Trained female runners under reported intake by 32 percent with no weight change, meaning the food was going in and simply was not being recorded.
PMID 16863723Digital tracking tools do not solve it.
When someone says "I am eating 1,400 calories and not losing", the gold standard literature says the true figure is very likely 1,900 to 2,100. She is not lying. This is unconscious, it is universal, and it is worst in exactly the people who most want to be getting it right.

Sleep

PMC29512875.5 h vs 8.5 h sleep under identical caloric restriction. Short sleep cut the proportion of weight lost as fat by 55 percent.
PMID 28545315Women sleeping 6 to 8 h lost 7.9 lb of fat vs 4.4 lb in short sleepers. Under 6 h carried more than four times the odds of a poor outcome.
PMID 41889166Short sleepers lost 4.5 percent less weight at 12 months.
PMC609203626 percent of 982 perimenopausal women met criteria for chronic insomnia, mostly trouble staying asleep rather than falling asleep.

On 9 or more hours. There is no case for telling a healthy person to sleep less. A meta-analysis of nearly 200,000 people found short sleep predicted future obesity and long sleep contributed nothing. A later meta-analysis found no significant association between long sleep and weight gain, BMI change or weight change. In women the positive correlation appears only above 12 hours. The mortality signal at the long end is dominated by reverse causation: long sleep is a marker of depression, inflammation and undiagnosed sleep disordered breathing rather than a cause of them.

But time in bed is not sleep, and this is where it matters for you. Perimenopausal sleep shows reduced efficiency, more waking in the night and more fragmentation, and lower estradiol tracks with more night waking. Nine hours in bed at 75 percent efficiency is 6.75 hours of sleep, which puts you on the short sleep side of the trial above while believing sleep is handled. Separately, sleep apnea prevalence climbs sharply after menopause, approaches 50 percent, is driven by visceral fat independently of BMI, and is systematically underdiagnosed in women. If you need 9 hours and still wake unrefreshed, that is a conversation with your doctor.

Training dose

PMID 27433992Graded dose response to weekly set volume, roughly +0.37 percent muscle growth per additional weekly set.
PMID 16287373Untrained individuals gain maximally at around 60 percent of one rep max, 3 days a week, about 4 sets per muscle group.
PMID 12618576Supporting dose response data for the same finding.
PMC1180142170 women, pre, peri and postmenopausal. Resistance training improved strength and balance irrespective of menopause status. Menopause did not blunt the training response.
That last one is the finding to hold on to. Your body still adapts to training the way it always did.

Building lean mass on purpose (step 7, version 2)

PMID 32569127RCT, 58 postmenopausal women, 12 weeks, supervised lower body training. 6 sets per exercise produced 6.1 percent leg lean mass against 2.3 percent for 3 sets (p < 0.001). One rep max gains were the same in both groups. Volume drives hypertrophy, not strength.
PMID 38744142Meta-analysis, 14 RCTs, postmenopausal and older women. Higher volume training added about 2.9 lb of lean mass against 2.0 lb for lower volume (Hedges' g 0.52 vs 0.34). GRADE certainty is low, so treat the direction as sound and the size as approximate.
PMID 37914977Randomized, 8 weeks, trained lifters on maintenance calories vs a 5 percent surplus vs a 15 percent surplus. No group difference in muscle thickness or squat one rep max. The 15 percent group gained more skinfold thickness, and rate of bodyweight gain predicted fat gain (R² = 0.49). Bigger surpluses buy fat, not muscle.
PMID 1999701912 weeks of resistance training in young women. Lean mass rose 4.2 lb in the arm drinking milk after training, against 2.4 lb on an equal calorie carbohydrate drink. Fat fell 3.5 lb in the milk arm only, and bodyweight moved about 1 lb. This is the source of the 2 to 4 lb per 12 weeks rate expectation, and it is a young population, so perimenopausal rates should be planned at the slower end.
Verdict: when ALMI is the limiting number, add sets before you add calories. The surplus only needs to be big enough to stop the deficit blocking recovery.

Honesty

Where this review is thin

Stated plainly so it is not over trusted. A review that claims no weak points is not a review.

  • One widely repeated claim about visceral fat rising from 8 percent to 23 percent of body fat through the transition is directionally consistent with the SWAN redistribution finding, but the original citation was not traced. It is not quoted anywhere on the main page for that reason.
  • A claim that combining hormone therapy with resistance training significantly improves bone density beyond training alone is attributed to a LIFTMOR family trial. LIFTMOR itself was not a hormone therapy trial, so treat this as an open question rather than a finding.
  • No dedicated search was run on alcohol or on daily non exercise activity specifically, both of which are plausibly relevant here.
  • The SWAN cohort is United States based and predominantly reports Black, Chinese, Japanese and White women. Generalising beyond that is an assumption.
  • Every effect size on these pages is a population average. Individual variation is wide, and the coaching still has to respond to the person in front of us rather than to the average.
The bottom line

The two camps in this field disagree loudly and publicly, and they agree on almost everything that matters. Lift. Eat more protein. Sleep. Move more during the day. Hold a small deficit. Take creatine.

The arguments are real but they live in the last ten percent. Nothing on the main page depends on which camp turns out to be right.
